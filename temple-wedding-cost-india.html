What does a temple wedding in India cost? Where the money actually goes

People assume the temple is the expensive part. It almost never is. The temple charges a slot fee; the guests, the nights, the feast and the travel decide the budget.

By The Temple WeddingUpdated 2026-10-30Reading time 7 min

1. What the temple itself charges

Across the temples that host weddings, the temple's own charge is modest and buys a slot, sometimes a priest, and a receipt. The figures below are the ones we could trace to an official source or a newspaper; everything else online comes from pilgrimage blogs that disagree with one another, and we treat those as "confirm with the office".

The pattern is clear: outside a handful of premium halls, the temple fee is a rounding error in a wedding budget.

2. What actually drives the cost

Five lines decide almost everything, and all of them scale with the guest list:

  1. Nights of stay. Guest count multiplied by nights multiplied by the room rate. Heritage towns like Hampi and Guruvayur have limited good inventory, and auspicious dates are the same for everyone, so rates and availability tighten sharply in season. A two-night wedding for 150 guests is a very different number from a one-night wedding for 60.
  2. Food. A sattvic banana-leaf feast is priced per leaf per meal, and a temple wedding usually means four to six meals across the stay, not one. Traditional cooks who know the regional menu cost more than a hotel buffet and are worth it.
  3. Travel. Airport and railhead transfers, coaches to the temple timed to the muhurta, and ghat-road or highway convoys for Kukke, Triyuginarayan or Hampi.
  4. Rituals. Purohits for the full sequence, their dakshina, samagri sourced to the temple's own mandates, nadaswaram or chenda, and a mantapa for the pre-wedding rituals when the temple only permits the main rite.
  5. Photography and attire. Photographers who work within temple rules, makeup artists at 3:30 am, silks and flower jewellery.

Décor is deliberately small at a temple wedding because temples permit little inside: flowers, mango leaves, banana stems and lamps at the hall. That is one of the real savings of this kind of wedding compared with a venue wedding.

3. What the temple fee never includes

At every temple we researched, the booking explicitly excludes the purohit (sometimes included at Kateel and TTD), nadaswaram, garlands, chairs, décor, catering and photography. Food inside the complex is almost never allowed: Vadapalani bars outside food, Hampi bars cooking, Bhoga Nandeeshwara sends meals to the community hall, and the Madras High Court has asked HR&CE to keep dining outside temples. The feast therefore needs a hall or hotel, and that hall is a separate booking with its own rate.

Questions

Is a temple wedding cheaper than a hotel wedding?

The ceremony is far cheaper, because the temple charges little and permits little décor. The stay, food and travel for guests cost the same as any destination wedding, and can cost more in small heritage towns with limited hotels. The honest answer is that a temple wedding moves money from décor and venue into rituals, guests and travel.

Does the temple fee include the priest?

Sometimes. TTD's Kalyana Vedika provides the priest free. Kateel's enrolment fee includes a purohit for a short ceremony. Most Tamil Nadu temples and all Karnataka trust halls expect you to bring your own purohit, which is what we arrange, matched to your community's sampradaya.

Why can't you give a ballpark figure?

Because the temple is not the variable. Tell us the guest count, the number of nights and the temple and we can give you a realistic range on the first call, before you pay anything.

Are there free temple weddings?

Yes. TTD's Kalyana Vedika at Tirumala is free for eligible Hindu couples, Dharmasthala runs an annual free mass marriage, and Karnataka's Saptapadi scheme and several Tamil Nadu temples hold mass weddings. These are simple, shared ceremonies with fixed formats, not planned weddings.
