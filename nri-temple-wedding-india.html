Planning from abroad

A temple wedding in India when you live abroad: NRIs, OCIs and foreign partners

Most of our families live outside India. The temple does not care where you live; it cares that you are Hindu, of age, with parents present, and that your paperwork is in order. The rest is time zones and logistics, which is our job.

1. Who can marry where

NRIs with Indian passports are treated like any Indian family at every temple: Hindu, 18 and 21, parents present, dossier in hand. OCI and PIO cardholders are foreign nationals in law, and this matters at Tamil Nadu HR&CE temples, where the minister's stated policy is weddings for "Hindus and Indian nationals" (The Hindu); in practice executive officers have discretion, and we ask in writing before booking. TTD, Guruvayur, the Karnataka trusts, ISKCON and the Uttarakhand temples ask about faith, not passports. Foreign nationals who are Hindu (converts, or Hindus of Nepali, Sri Lankan, Mauritian, Fijian or Caribbean heritage) can marry at Guruvayur with a Hindu declaration, at TTD, at Triyuginarayan (which markets itself to international couples), and at ISKCON temples.

2. When one partner is not Hindu by birth

This is the question we get most from overseas families, and the honest answer is that almost no temple will perform the wedding unless both parties are Hindu. Guruvayur has demanded an Arya Samaj conversion certificate before admitting a convert (Kerala Kaumudi); Tamil Nadu temples cancel non-Hindu weddings; and a Shimla temple hall that took a Muslim booking in 2026 ended up permitting a reception only, with no religious rites. The workable routes are:

  • Conversion through Arya Samaj (a shuddhi ceremony and certificate), after which temples such as Guruvayur accept the partner as Hindu. This is a personal decision, and we will not push it; we will tell you which temples accept it.
  • ISKCON temples (Bengaluru, Delhi Dwarka, Mumbai Juhu, Vrindavan), which conduct Vedic weddings in their own halls with in-house sattvic catering and do not apply the birth-Hindu test the way state temples do.
  • An Arya Samaj mandir wedding, which is a Vedic rite with a certificate that registrars accept, followed by a blessing visit to the family's temple.
  • A family-temple wedding for the Hindu rites and a civil marriage under the Special Marriage Act for the legal record, which also sidesteps conversion entirely. The temple visit then becomes a darshan and ashirvada, not the solemnisation.

Guest lists matter too. Non-Hindu guests cannot enter Guruvayur, Puri or Padmanabhaswamy at all, and Tamil Nadu temples admit them only on a written undertaking beyond the flagstaff since the Madras High Court's 2024 Palani order (SCC Online). If half your friends are flying in from abroad, Bhoga Nandeeshwara, ISKCON, Triyuginarayan and the Karnataka trusts are kinder venues than Guruvayur.

3. Documents from abroad

  • Passports for both, plus OCI cards where held; Aadhaar for parents resident in India
  • Age proof (passport suffices)
  • An unmarried certificate or a sworn affidavit of single status. Indian temples want this from a revenue office; for NRIs, a notarised affidavit, or a single-status certificate from your country of residence, is what we present with the application
  • Proof of Hindu faith where the temple asks: a parent's declaration, or an Arya Samaj certificate for converts
  • Parental consent letters where parents cannot travel
  • Passport photographs; the printed invitation
A note on visas: OCI cardholders need nothing extra. Foreign-passport guests enter on an e-Tourist visa, which is fine for attending a wedding. A foreign national who is the bride or groom should also enter on a tourist visa; there is no "marriage visa" category, and the ceremony does not change immigration status.

5. Making the marriage count abroad

A temple receipt is evidence of solemnisation, never a marriage certificate. For a US green card, a UK spouse visa or a Singapore dependant pass you need the state-issued marriage certificate from registration under the Hindu Marriage Act (Kerala within 45 days, Tamil Nadu under its compulsory 2009 Act, Karnataka through Kaveri 2.0, and at Tirumala on the day at the registrar inside the Kalyana Vedika). For use abroad, that certificate is then apostilled by the Ministry of External Affairs (India is a Hague Apostille Convention member), which we arrange through an authorised outsourcing agency, typically a one to two week process. Keep the wedding photographs of the saptapadi and thali-tying: Indian courts have held that a certificate alone does not prove a Hindu marriage where the rites were not performed (Scroll), and foreign consulates sometimes ask for them.

Questions

Can we plan the whole wedding without visiting India first?

Yes. Every family we have worked with from abroad planned entirely on video and arrived three days before the wedding. We do the temple office visits, the hotel inspections and the food tastings, and send you recordings and photographs.

Can parents who cannot travel give consent remotely?

Most temples want at least one parent or an elder guardian physically present with ID. Where a parent cannot travel, a notarised consent letter and an attending elder usually satisfy the counter; we confirm per temple in writing.

Will our foreign friends be allowed into the temple?

It depends on the temple. Guruvayur, Puri and Padmanabhaswamy bar non-Hindus; Tamil Nadu temples require a signed undertaking; Karnataka trusts, ISKCON, Triyuginarayan and Bhoga Nandeeshwara welcome guests of any faith who follow the dress code. We pick the venue with your guest list in mind.

How do we get the marriage certificate recognised in the US or UK?

Register under the Hindu Marriage Act in the state where you married, then have the certificate apostilled by India's Ministry of External Affairs. We handle both and courier the originals to you.
