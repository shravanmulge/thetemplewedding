How to get married in a temple in India: eligibility, documents, booking and registration

Every temple counter we have dealt with asks for the same dossier and enforces the same three rules. Get those right and the rest is logistics.

By The Temple WeddingUpdated 2026-10-30Reading time 8 min

1. Who is eligible

Three conditions are near-universal at Indian temples that host weddings. Both parties must be Hindu. Tamil Nadu's HR&CE minister has stated that only Hindu Indian nationals may marry in the state's temples, and an executive officer in Chennai cancelled a scheduled Christian wedding in 2023 on exactly that ground (Organiser). The bride must be 18 and the groom 21, the statutory ages under the Hindu Marriage Act, and counters check documents; Odisha's Endowments Commission ordered age checks at every shrine in 2021. Parents are expected to be present, and most temples treat the wedding as an arranged first marriage: TTD will not conduct "love marriages and second marriages", Dharmasthala bars second marriages, and Tamil Nadu temples require a parent to sign the register (Hans India). Inter-caste arranged marriages are accepted at most Tamil Nadu and Karnataka temples with a police no-objection certificate and notarised parental consent.

2. The document dossier

Prepare one set and it will satisfy every counter we have seen, and it is reused at civil registration:

  • Aadhaar (or passport for NRIs) for the bride, the groom and all four parents
  • Age proof for both: birth certificate, SSLC/10th certificate or passport
  • An unmarried certificate or affidavit. In Tamil Nadu this is now the e-Sevai "Unmarried Certificate" (service REV-120), which HR&CE accepts in place of the old VAO "first marriage certificate" (The Hindu); at Tirumala it comes from the MRO
  • The wedding invitation, once printed
  • Passport-size photographs of both
  • Community certificate where the temple asks (several Tamil Nadu temples do)
  • Police NOC and notarised parental consent for inter-caste marriages, where applicable
  • ID for two or three witnesses

Two things to understand about the booking itself. First, the temple's own charge is usually small, from a few hundred rupees to a few thousand, and covers the slot and sometimes the priest; everything else (purohit, nadaswaram, garlands, chairs, décor, food) is yours to arrange. Second, the inside-the-temple ceremony is short and quota-limited: two or three weddings a day at Madurai Meenakshi, one or two at Mylapore Kapaleeswarar, thirty minutes at Srirangam. The crowd goes to a hall. See our cost guide for what that means for a budget.

4. What happens on the day

You report early, often before dawn, with the dossier and the receipt. Dress is dhoti or veshti for the groom and saree for the bride; footwear comes off; Western clothes and shorts are turned away at many temples. Photography is limited: Guruvayur sells a separate photo/video ticket and caps crews at two photographers and two videographers; Madurai and Chennai temples require authorised photographers and ban tripods and drones; ASI sites treat any shoot as commercial filming. No cooking, no outside food, no alcohol and no DJ inside the complex, and décor at the ceremony point is flowers only. The feast and reception happen at a hall or hotel, which is where most of the planning effort goes. The temple issues a receipt or certificate of solemnisation, which is evidence that the rite happened, not a legal marriage certificate.

5. Registering the marriage

A Hindu marriage is valid when solemnised with the customary rites under section 7 of the Hindu Marriage Act 1955 (complete at the seventh step where saptapadi is part of the rite), and it becomes provable through registration under state rules. The Supreme Court held in Dolly Rani (2024) that a registration certificate proves nothing if the ceremonies were never performed (Scroll), so keep the photographs of the saptapadi and the thali-tying as evidence, not just keepsakes. State windows differ: Kerala's Common Rules 2008 require a memorandum within 45 days with the temple's certificate and two witnesses; Tamil Nadu's 2009 Act makes registration compulsory with the priest as a signatory; Karnataka registers through Kaveri 2.0 with three witnesses; Uttarakhand and Rajasthan have compulsory-registration Acts. Tirumala is the exception that makes life easy: a Hindu Marriage Sub-Registrar sits inside the Kalyana Vedika complex and registers the marriage the same day with age proof, residence proof, a wedding photo, the receipt, the invitation and three witnesses (The Federal). We build registration into every wedding we plan.

Questions

Is a temple wedding legally valid in India?

Yes, if it is solemnised with the customary rites of the parties under the Hindu Marriage Act. Validity does not depend on registration, but you need registration to prove the marriage for passports, visas, bank accounts and property. Register within your state's window, typically 45 to 90 days.

Can we marry in a temple without our parents?

At most temples that host weddings, no. Counters ask for both sets of parents' ID and a parent's signature, and several temples state they do not host marriages without family consent. Court-marriage certificates do not change this: temples in Guwahati refused couples who had them.

Do temples accept inter-caste marriages?

Most Tamil Nadu and Karnataka temples do, as arranged marriages with parents present, a police NOC and notarised consent. A few restrict weddings to one community. We check the specific temple's rule before you commit.

How early do we need to arrive?

Pre-dawn for most muhurtas. Guruvayur weddings start around 4 to 5 am; TTD asks couples to report six hours before the muhurta. We time hotel wake-up calls and convoys accordingly.
